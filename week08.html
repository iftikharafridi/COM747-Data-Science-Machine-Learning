Clustering and PCA

Unsupervised learning finds structure without labels. Group similar cases with k-means and hierarchical clustering, decide how many groups there are, and squeeze many variables into a few with principal component analysis.

01

What kinds of callers use a helpline?

Ulster researchers took 61,287 callers to a mental health helpline, described each by three features (number of calls, mean call length, variation in call length), standardised them and ran k-means. Five caller types emerged, from a tiny group of very prolific callers to the majority who call 5–6 times briefly. No one labelled the callers in advance: the clusters created the labels. That is unsupervised learning.

  • LO2Describe the k-means algorithm step by step and run it with kmeans().
  • LO3Choose k with the elbow method, and explain why features must be scaled.

02

Why cluster?

Applications

Customer segmentation, types of app users, patient phenotypes, anomaly detection, creating labels for later supervised learning (semi-supervised), exploratory analysis.

Hard vs soft

Hard: each case in exactly one cluster (k-means). Soft: a probability of belonging to each (Gaussian mixtures). Other methods: k-medoids, DBSCAN (density), hierarchical.

Choosing features

Which features characterise cases? Ask domain experts. Prefer independent features with real variance and little missing data. Categorical features need one-hot encoding (novice / amateur / professional → three 0/1 columns).

Distance

Euclidean by default. Manhattan (city-block) and Minkowski are alternatives. Every distance-based method needs features on comparable scales.

03

k-means step by step

Imagine asking people in a gym how far they can run and how far they can swim. k-means: (1) pick k starting centroids at random; (2) assign each person to the nearest centroid; (3) update each centroid to the mean of its members; repeat 2–3 until nothing changes. It minimises the total within-cluster sum of squares (WSS).

Explorer

Run k-means one step at a time

Shaded regions show which centroid is nearest everywhere (a Voronoi diagram). Different random starts can end in different solutions; that is why R's kmeans(..., nstart = 25) tries 25 starts and keeps the best.

04

How many clusters?

WSS always falls as k grows (with k = n every point is its own cluster). Look for the elbow: the k after which adding clusters stops helping much. Other guides: a business constraint ("we can afford 3 campaigns"), the rule of thumb k ≈ √(n/2), or the silhouette method.

05

Why scaling matters

Explorer

mtcars: miles per gallon and horsepower

k-means with k = 3. On raw values horsepower is ten times larger than mpg, so distances are almost entirely horsepower: the clusters are vertical bands that ignore fuel economy. After scale() both features count equally.

06

Naming the mtcars clusters

mtcarsNew <- scale(mtcars[, c(1, 2, 4)])        # mpg, cyl, hp
set.seed(123)
kMeansClust <- kmeans(mtcarsNew, centers = 3, iter.max = 10, nstart = 25)
aggregate(mtcars[, c(1, 2, 4)], by = list(cluster = kMeansClust$cluster), mean)
ClusterSizemean mpgmean cylindersmean hpLabel we give it
The centroids (cluster means) are archetypes. Giving them names turns clusters into new knowledge. The helpline study did the same: "prolific callers", "single lengthy support callers", "not availing of service".
07

Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative clustering starts with every case on its own and repeatedly merges the two closest groups. The result is a dendrogram: clusters within clusters. Cutting it at a height gives a flat clustering, so you can see several values of k at once.

Explorer

Cut the mtcars dendrogram

hc <- hclust(dist(mtcarsNew)); plot(hc); cutree(hc, h = …). Complete linkage: the distance between two groups is the distance between their farthest members.

08

Principal component analysis

With many variables we can't plot the data, and we need more cases to cover the space (the curse of dimensionality). PCA rotates the axes so the first new axis, PC1, points along the direction of greatest variance, PC2 the next greatest at right angles, and so on. Keeping the first few components reduces dimensions while losing little information. Mathematically, the components are the eigenvectors of the correlation matrix, ordered by their eigenvalues.

Explorer

Find the direction of most variance

variance along this axis
share of total variance

Each point is projected onto the axis (thin lines). PC1 is the angle where the projections are most spread out. With two standardised variables correlated at r, PC1 explains (1 + r) / 2 of the variance.

09

PCA on the iris data

irisPCA <- prcomp(iris[, 1:4], center = TRUE, scale. = TRUE)
summary(irisPCA)     # PC1 73.0%, PC2 22.9%: two components keep 95.8% of the information
biplot(irisPCA)

    wssplot <- function(data, nc = 15, seed = 12) {
  wss <- (nrow(data) - 1) * sum(apply(data, 2, var))   # k = 1: total sum of squares
  for (i in 2:nc) { set.seed(seed); wss[i] <- sum(kmeans(data, centers = i)$withinss) }

Key terms

TermMeaning
CentroidThe mean position of a cluster's members; its archetype.
WSSWithin-cluster sum of squares: total squared distance from points to their centroid.
Elbow methodChoose k where adding clusters stops reducing WSS much.
Voronoi diagramRegions of space closest to each centroid.
DendrogramA tree showing the order in which clusters merge.
Curse of dimensionalityMore variables need far more cases; distances become less meaningful.
Principal componentA new axis: a weighted mix of the original variables.
LoadingThe weight of an original variable in a component.
Scree plotVariance explained by each component, in order.
