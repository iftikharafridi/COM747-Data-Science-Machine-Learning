04

The normal distribution

Many measurements are roughly bell-shaped. A normal distribution is completely described by its mean (centre) and SD (spread). Probability is the shaded area under the curve; the whole area is 1.

FunctionAnswersExample
pnorm(q, mean, sd)P(X ≤ q): area to the leftpnorm(30, 50, 25) → 0.212
1 - pnorm(q, …)P(X > q): area to the right1 - pnorm(70, 55, 30) → 0.309

Shape: skewness and kurtosis

Skewness · symmetry

0 for a normal distribution. Positive: long right tail (salaries, house prices). Negative: long left tail (an easy exam).

Kurtosis · tails

3 for a normal distribution. Above 3: heavy tails, more extreme values (leptokurtic). Below 3: light tails (platykurtic).

Explorer

Skew a distribution

The mean is dragged towards the long tail; the median stays nearer the peak. For skewed data report median and IQR and use non-parametric tests. A log() transform often makes right-skewed data close to normal.

06

Standardisation and z-scores

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean: z = (x − mean) / sd. It is metric agnostic, so scores on different scales (a quiz out of 15, an exam out of 100) become comparable and can be added into a composite score. In R, scale(x) standardises a whole vector.

Explorer

z-score converter

z
percentile
sigma 16z+50
Hull 14z+50
T 10z+50

Defaults: a weight of 84 kg from set.seed(1); weight <- round(rnorm(50, 90, 10)) (mean 91, SD 8.33). The sigma, Hull and T scales turn z into positive scores for reports.

Min–max normalisation rescales to 0–1 instead: (x - min(x)) / (max(x) - min(x)). Machine learning methods that use distances (k-NN, k-means) need one of these.
07

The central limit theorem

Take many random samples of size n and calculate each sample's mean. When n is large (a common rule: n ≥ 30), the means follow a normal distribution, even if the population does not. This is why so many statistical tests assume normality.

Is my variable normal? Look first (histogram, qqnorm()), then test: shapiro.test(x). H0: the data are normal. p < 0.05 means reject normality. rundata$FirstRun: p = 0.37 (normal is reasonable). student_data$G3: p < 0.001 (not normal).

Exploratory data analysis on real data

student_data.csv has 395 students and 33 variables (UCI Student Performance). G1, G2 and G3 are grades out of 20 for each period; G3 is the final grade.

students <- read.csv("student_data.csv")
hist(students$G3, breaks = 0:20)
boxplot(G3 ~ sex, data = students)
tapply(students$G3, students$sex, mean)   # F 9.97, M 10.91
sum(students$G3 == 0)                     # 38
cor(students$G1, students$G3)             # 0.80

Key terms

TermMeaning
Random variableA variable whose values come from a random process.
Probability distributionLinks every outcome (or range) with its probability.
Cumulative probabilityP(X ≤ x): the chance of a value up to x. pnorm(), pbinom().
BinAn interval used to group continuous values in a histogram.
DensityA smooth curve whose area between two values is a probability.
z-scoreDistance from the mean in standard deviations.
Skewness / kurtosisAsymmetry (0 if normal) / heaviness of the tails (3 if normal).
Central limit theoremMeans of large samples are approximately normal.
EDAExploratory data analysis: summarising and plotting data before modelling.
