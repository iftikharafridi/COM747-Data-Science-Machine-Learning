Teamwork Reflection and Process Mining

Two things to take away from the module. First, how to turn your group project into strong answers at a job interview. Second, process mining: discovering the real journeys people take through a service from its event logs, with the bupaR packages in R. Plus a guest talk applying regression and clustering to the quality of health apps.

01

"Tell me about a time you worked in a team"

Almost every graduate interview asks it, and most candidates answer vaguely. You have just done a group data science project, so you have real material: a goal, decisions, disagreements, a result. This week you turn that into evidence. Then we look at a technique that is all about journeys and sequences, process mining, which analyses the timestamps a system already records to show how people really move through a service.

  • LO1Reflect on the benefits and challenges of team-based projects.
  • LO2Structure an interview answer with evidence of your own contribution (STAR).
  • LO3Describe an event log: case, activity, timestamp, resource.
  • LO4Read a process map (absolute and relative frequencies) and a trace explorer; relate them to a first-order Markov model.
  • LO5Use bupaR to compute activity frequencies, trace lengths, process maps and traces.

03

Answering the interview questions

The two questions from the sheet: "Give an example of how you positively contributed to a group-based project" and "What are the benefits and challenges of working as part of a team, and how might you resolve competing ideas between members?" Interviewers want a specific example, your actions ("I", not "we"), and a measurable result. The STAR structure gives you that: Situation, Task, Action, Result, plus a line of reflection.

05

Process mining: event logs

Every time a hospital registers a patient, books an X-ray or discharges someone, a system writes a timestamped record. Process mining reads these event logs and automatically discovers the pathways people actually take. It can discover the real process, check conformance to a standard pathway, and find deviations and bottlenecks. An event log needs at least:

Case ID

Whose journey this is: a patient, an order, a helpline caller.

Activity

What happened: Registration, Triage, X-Ray, Check-out.

Timestamp

When it happened, so events can be ordered. Often a start and a complete time.

The first events of the first two cases in the patients event log from the eventdataR package (500 patients, 2,721 activity instances, 7 activities). A trace is the sequence of activities for one case.

06

Process maps

A process map is a directed graph (Week 11): activities are nodes, and an arc from A to B counts how often B directly followed A. Showing the relative frequency on each arc gives the probability of the next activity given the current one: a first-order Markov model.

Explorer

process_map()

Click an activity to see its outgoing probabilities. Arc thickness shows frequency. Node shading shows how many cases reach it. The therapy log is invented, in the style of the study of a psychological therapies service by Potts et al. (2023): baseline severity, number of sessions, outcome.

The sheet's question: what does 18.38% on Registration mean? With frequency("relative"), a node shows its share of all activity instances: Registration happened 500 times out of 2,721 activity instances in the log, and 500 / 2721 = 0.1838. The arcs show the share of the from activity's departures that go each way.
07

Traces and trace length

The most common trace: Registration → Triage and Assessment → X-Ray → Discuss Results → Check-out (258 patients, 51.6%). The second: Registration → Triage and Assessment → Blood test → MRI SCAN → Discuss Results → Check-out (234, 46.8%). You can confirm both on the process map.
08

Guest talk: what makes a good health app?

A PhD study with ORCHA (an organisation that reviews health apps) analysed assessments of over 2,000 apps. Each app gets a score from 0 to 100 on professional/clinical assurance (PCA), user experience (UX) and data privacy (DP). Methods you know: Spearman correlation, multiple regression with a Bonferroni-corrected alpha (Week 3's multiple testing), the elbow method and k-medoids clustering (like k-means, but each centre is a real app).

Study 1: quality vs ratings and downloads

Spearman correlations between the ORCHA score and user ratings (−0.024, p = .47) and download level (−0.011, p = .75) were tiny and not significant (Bonferroni α = .05 / 9 ≈ .006). Regressions explained about 1% of the variance at most. No link between quality and what users rate or download.

Study 2: four types of app

k-medoids on 1,402 apps (k = 4 from the elbow plot) found: apps with poor user ratings; apps with poor PCA and DP; apps with poor PCA; and higher-quality apps with higher ratings. Again, ratings didn't track the quality scores.

Key terms

TermMeaning
STARSituation, Task, Action, Result: a structure for evidence-based answers.
Event logTimestamped records of activities for each case.
CaseOne journey through the process (a patient, an order).
TraceThe ordered sequence of activities for a case.
Process mapDirected graph of activities and the transitions between them.
Conformance checkingComparing real traces with a standard or ideal pathway.
First-order Markov modelThe next state depends only on the current state.
Throughput timeTime from a case's first to last event.
k-medoidsLike k-means, but each cluster centre is an actual case.
